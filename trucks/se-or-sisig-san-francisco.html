San Francisco, California · Featured Truck

Señor Sisig

Cuisine: Filipino-Mexican
Signature: Sisig burrito
Home base: San Francisco, California
Rolling since 2010

Señor Sisig never chased a trend; it chased its regulars, and caught them. The Filipino-Mexican kitchen out of San Francisco, California runs on repeat orders and first-name greetings at the window.

Señor Sisig has been rolling since 2010. San Francisco's Filipino-Mexican pioneer, credited with putting the sisig burrito on the national map.

sisig burrito: that is the signature at Señor Sisig, and it makes the whole argument for the truck in a single order. This is the dish San Francisco crosses town for.

Few cities run a truck scene as deep as San Francisco's, and Señor Sisig holds its corner of it through every rhythm the city keeps — office lunches, evening lots, festival weekends when the whole fleet turns out.

The kitchen at Señor Sisig treats Filipino-Mexican as a craft of constraints: a small grill, a shorter ticket, nowhere to hide. What comes through the window is the argument that constraints, respected, make cooking better.

Featured entries in this directory are the trucks other trucks get measured against, and Señor Sisig earned that standing in San Francisco the only way it can be earned — year after year of services where the food matched the reputation.

Ask the regulars why they come back and the answers stack up: the cooking, yes, and the remembering — names, usual orders, how you take it. Señor Sisig runs on that kind of memory.

Señor Sisig does not need a theme song or a mascot. Its identity is written in three facts — San Francisco, Filipino-Mexican, sisig burrito — and in the regulars who would spot a substitute in one bite.

Ask a San Francisco local to name the trucks that matter and Señor Sisig comes early in the list — not out of habit, but out of a decade of lunches that landed right.

Señor Sisig is recorded here with its home base, its cuisine, and its signature, as part of the permanent record this directory keeps. Trucks hold their lines the same way they earned them — by still being the trucks their cities say they are — and the quarterly review keeps the record honest.
